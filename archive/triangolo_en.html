What happens with the triangle

Squares and hexagons tile the plane perfectly, which is why they lend themselves to building a spiral of numbers. The equilateral triangle is the third and last regular polygon capable of doing the same — let's see what happens to prime numbers on this grid.

How it's built

Start from a central point (the number 1) and move along six possible directions, each 60° from the last — these are the natural directions of a triangular lattice, where every point has exactly six neighbors. The first ring around the center has 6 points, the second 12, the third 18: each ring always adds 6 more points than its radius.

Connecting every point to its neighbors automatically produces a mesh of equilateral triangles — the original tiling reappears on its own, without needing to be drawn separately.

What to look for

With Ulam's square spiral, prime numbers tended to line up along the diagonals. Here the lattice has a different symmetry — six main directions instead of four — so it's worth watching whether a similar pattern emerges along one or more of the six lattice directions as you reveal the primes.

The underlying mechanism is the same as before: moving in a straight line across a lattice always amounts to evaluating some polynomial (linear or quadratic) in the distance traveled, and some of these polynomials produce prime numbers more often than others.

A closer look

Why exactly these three shapes

Only three regular polygons tile the plane without leaving gaps: the equilateral triangle, the square, and the hexagon. This has been known since ancient Greece, and it comes down to each polygon's interior angle: for identical copies to fit perfectly around a point, the interior angle has to divide 360° exactly. That only happens for 60° (triangle), 90° (square), and 120° (hexagon).

Why the pentagon doesn't work

A regular pentagon's interior angle is 108°, and 360° isn't a whole multiple of 108°. So to build something analogous with a pentagon, you'd either have to give up regularity (as in the Cairo pentagonal tiling) or give up true tiling altogether, using instead a radiating structure with the same 5-fold symmetry.

A coincidence worth noting

The lattice above stops at exactly the thirteenth ring, for a total of:

1 + 3 · 13 · 14 = 547 punti

And purely by chance, 547 is itself a prime number — in fact, it's exactly the 101st prime number overall. The ring closes, then, right at the moment the prime counter passes 100.
