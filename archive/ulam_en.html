The pattern hiding inside prime numbers

In 1963, bored during a conference, mathematician Stanisław Ulam doodled the integers in a spiral on a sheet of paper. What he saw changed the way people looked at prime numbers ever since.

How it's built

Write out the integers 1, 2, 3, 4… arranged in a spiral, starting at the center and winding outward. No trick involved: the numbers simply follow their natural order, bent into this geometric shape instead of lined up in a row.

Then color every cell that contains a prime number, leaving the rest neutral. That's exactly what you can do yourself above, one number at a time.

What jumps out

If prime numbers were distributed truly at random, you'd expect to see them scattered evenly across the grid, with no recognizable pattern. That's not what happens: as you keep revealing them, they tend to line up along clearly visible diagonals, some much denser than others.

Ulam noticed it by chance, drawing a few hundred numbers by hand. When the same pattern was later generated by computer across millions of numbers, the diagonals were still there, just as crisp as he'd first seen them on paper.

A closer look

Discovered out of boredom

Ulam was sitting through a talk he later described as "long and not very interesting." To pass the time, he started writing the integers in a spiral on graph paper, circling the primes as he spotted them. What struck him immediately was that the circles weren't scattered randomly at all — they kept lining up along diagonal lines, insistently.

Why the diagonals form

Each diagonal of the spiral corresponds to the values of a quadratic polynomial of the form:

f(n) = 4n² + bn + c

as n varies, for particular pairs of coefficients b and c. Some of these polynomials, for reasons rooted in number theory, produce a prime number far more often than expected. The most famous case is the polynomial discovered by Euler long before Ulam's spiral:

n² + n + 41

which returns a prime number for every n from 0 to 39 in a row — an almost comical case of arithmetic generosity. The densest diagonals in the Ulam spiral correspond to exactly this kind of polynomial, particularly "lucky" at producing primes.

Still an open problem

We can explain why some quadratic polynomials produce more primes than others, and we can even calculate how dense the diagonals ought to be, thanks to a prediction known as Hardy and Littlewood's Conjecture F. But that prediction remains, precisely, a conjecture: no one has yet rigorously proven why the pattern Ulam noticed is exactly as pronounced as it is. It's one of many cases where prime numbers show an order that's visible to the naked eye, but not yet fully understood by mathematics.
