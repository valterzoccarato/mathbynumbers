How you actually calculate a million digits

π, √2, and the golden ratio all have infinitely many decimal digits that never repeat. Before asking what they mean — the history, the trivia, everything else — it's worth understanding how you actually extract them: by hand, with centuries-old logic, and with a computer, with today's.

By hand, with pen and paper

Long before computers, mathematicians already had solid methods for reaching tens or hundreds of digits — slow, tedious, but correct.

π

Archimedes' method

Around 250 BC, Archimedes trapped π between two regular polygons — one inscribed and one circumscribed around a circle. The more sides he added (he started at 6, went up to 96), the tighter the two polygons squeezed around the true value. With pen and paper, he managed to prove that π sits between 3.1408 and 3.1429.

√2

The Babylonian method

You start from any guess, and improve it by repeating a simple average: if your guess is too high, dividing the number you want the root of by that guess gives a value that's too low — the average of the two is already closer to the truth than either one. Repeat, and each time the number of correct digits roughly doubles.

φ

From Fibonacci ratios

Dividing two consecutive Fibonacci numbers, taking bigger and bigger ones (8/5, 13/8, 21/13...), the result gets closer and closer to the golden ratio. It's slow, but it only requires division — no square roots, no complicated formulas.

A human record: in 1873, William Shanks calculated 707 digits of π by hand — a project that took years. It wasn't until 1946 that someone noticed he'd made a calculation error, and every digit from the 528th onward was wrong. Nobody had caught it for nearly a century.

With a computer

The underlying principle doesn't change much — it's still about repeating a calculation that gets closer and closer to the true value. What changes is how fast you get there, and how far you can push it before rounding error becomes a problem.

Newton's method, for real

The Babylonian trick for square roots is actually a special case of a much more general idea, Newton's method: start from a guess, and at each step correct it using how much you're "off" in the right direction. Applied with a computer's arbitrary-precision arithmetic (numbers that can have millions of digits, not just the 15-17 a computer normally handles), Newton's method for square roots is quadratically convergent: the number of correct digits doubles with every single step.

xn+1 = ½ ( xn + N / xn )

With this formula, reaching a million correct digits of √2 takes only about twenty steps — the rest is entirely a matter of how fast the computer can multiply enormous numbers together, which is really the true bottleneck in these calculations.

Formulas that "eat" digits with every step

For π, today's world records don't use polygons: they use formulas discovered in the 20th century that converge far more rapidly. The most famous is the Chudnovsky formula (1988), a series where every single term adds about 14 new correct digits:

1/π = 12 · Σ [ (−1)ᵏ (6k)! (13591409 + 545140134k) ] / [ (3k)! (k!)³ 640320^(3k+3/2) ]

For the golden ratio, the calculation is almost a byproduct: φ = (1 + √5) / 2, so you just apply the same Newton's method used for √2 above to √5, then one sum and one division by 2.

How we calculated them for this site

The digits you can search and explore in the "Search for digits" section — ten million for each of the three constants — were generated following exactly these principles: arbitrary-precision arithmetic and rapidly-converging methods, not sixteen-hundred-sided polygons à la Archimedes.

In practice, √2 and φ were calculated with the Newton's method described above, pushed to the precision needed; π with a rapidly-converging algorithm from the same family as the Chudnovsky formula. The heavy lifting — multiplying together numbers with millions of digits — was handed off to numerical computing libraries built specifically for arbitrary-precision arithmetic, which do exactly this kind of work for a living.
