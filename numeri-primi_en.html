The pattern hiding inside prime numbers

In 1963, bored during a conference, mathematician Stanisław Ulam doodled the integers in a spiral on a sheet of paper — and noticed the primes weren't scattered randomly at all. Here you can explore the same phenomenon on two different lattices: Ulam's classic square, and a triangular lattice with six directions.

How it's built

Write out the integers 1, 2, 3, 4… arranged in a spiral, starting at the center and winding outward. No trick involved: the numbers simply follow their natural order, bent into this geometric shape instead of lined up in a row.

Then color every cell that contains a prime number, leaving the rest neutral. That's exactly what you can do yourself above, one number at a time.

What jumps out

If prime numbers were distributed truly at random, you'd expect to see them scattered evenly across the grid, with no recognizable pattern. That's not what happens: as you keep revealing them, they tend to line up along clearly visible diagonals, some much denser than others.

Ulam noticed it by chance, drawing a few hundred numbers by hand. When the same pattern was later generated by computer across millions of numbers, the diagonals were still there, just as crisp as he'd first seen them on paper.

How it's built

Start from a central point (the number 1) and move along six possible directions, each 60° from the last — these are the natural directions of a triangular lattice, where every point has exactly six neighbors. The first ring around the center has 6 points, the second 12, the third 18: each ring always adds 6 more points than its radius.

Connecting every point to its neighbors automatically produces a mesh of equilateral triangles — the original tiling reappears on its own, without needing to be drawn separately.

What to look for

With Ulam's square spiral, prime numbers tended to line up along the diagonals. Here the lattice has a different symmetry — six main directions instead of four — so it's worth watching whether a similar pattern emerges along one or more of the six lattice directions as you reveal the primes.

The underlying mechanism is the same as before: moving in a straight line across a lattice always amounts to evaluating some polynomial (linear or quadratic) in the distance traveled, and some of these polynomials produce prime numbers more often than others.

A closer look

Why exactly these shapes tile the plane

Only three regular polygons tile the plane without leaving gaps: the equilateral triangle, the square, and the hexagon. This has been known since ancient Greece, and it comes down to each polygon's interior angle: for identical copies to fit perfectly around a point, the interior angle has to divide 360° exactly. That only happens for 60° (triangle), 90° (square), and 120° (hexagon).

Why the pentagon doesn't work

A regular pentagon's interior angle is 108°, and 360° isn't a whole multiple of 108°. So to build something analogous with a pentagon, you'd either have to give up regularity (as in the Cairo pentagonal tiling) or give up true tiling altogether, using instead a radiating structure with the same 5-fold symmetry.

The square: discovered out of boredom

Ulam was sitting through a talk he later described as "long and not very interesting." To pass the time, he started writing the integers in a spiral on graph paper, circling the primes as he spotted them. What struck him immediately was that the circles weren't scattered randomly at all — they kept lining up along diagonal lines, insistently.

Why the diagonals form

Each diagonal of the square spiral corresponds to the values of a quadratic polynomial of the form:

f(n) = 4n² + bn + c

as n varies, for particular pairs of coefficients b and c. Some of these polynomials, for reasons rooted in number theory, produce a prime number far more often than expected. The most famous case is the polynomial discovered by Euler long before Ulam's spiral:

n² + n + 41

which returns a prime number for every n from 0 to 39 in a row — an almost comical case of arithmetic generosity. The densest diagonals in the square spiral correspond to exactly this kind of polynomial, particularly "lucky" at producing primes.

The triangle: a coincidence discovered along the way

The triangular lattice you explored above stops at exactly the thirteenth ring, for a total of:

1 + 3 · 13 · 14 = 547 punti

And purely by chance, 547 is itself a prime number — in fact, it's exactly the 101st prime number overall. The ring closes, then, right at the moment the prime counter passes 100.

Still an open problem

We can explain why some polynomials produce more primes than others on a given lattice, and we can even calculate how dense the diagonals or main directions ought to be, thanks to a prediction known as Hardy and Littlewood's Conjecture F. But that prediction remains, precisely, a conjecture: no one has yet rigorously proven why the patterns observed — on the square as on the triangle — are exactly as pronounced as they are. It's one of many cases where prime numbers show an order that's visible to the naked eye, but not yet fully understood by mathematics.
