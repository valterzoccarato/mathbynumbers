The sequence that draws a spiral

Fibonacci isn't just a list of numbers adding into each other. It's the pattern nature keeps choosing, again and again, to grow in the most efficient way possible.

How it's built

Start with two squares of side 1, placed side by side. Each following square has a side equal to the sum of the two previous ones — just like every number in the Fibonacci sequence is the sum of the two before it: 1, 1, 2, 3, 5, 8, 13, 21…

Connecting the corners of the squares with circular arcs, the curve that emerges approximates what's known as the golden spiral — the same shape that shows up, with surprising regularity, throughout nature.

Where it actually shows up

Nautilus shells

The inner chamber grows following this exact proportion, keeping the same shape as the animal gets bigger.

Sunflower seeds

The seeds arrange themselves in crossing spirals, and the number of spirals in each direction is almost always a Fibonacci number.

Galaxy arms

Many spiral galaxies, including the Milky Way, follow a logarithmic curve remarkably close to the golden one, at a cosmic scale.

A closer look

Where it comes from

The name comes from Leonardo Fibonacci, a mathematician from Pisa who published the Liber Abaci in 1202. The problem that introduces the sequence is almost comical: how many pairs of rabbits do you end up with after a year, starting from a single pair, if every adult pair produces a new pair each month and every new pair becomes fertile after one month? The answer, month by month, is 1, 1, 2, 3, 5, 8, 13… The sequence had already appeared in Indian mathematics centuries earlier, but Fibonacci's name is the one that stuck in the West.

The rule that generates it

The definition is a recurrence relation:

F(1) = 1,  F(2) = 1,  F(n) = F(n−1) + F(n−2)

Simple to write down, yet it generates behavior that shows up everywhere in nature — the simplicity of the rule doesn't mean the results are simple at all.

Why the ratio converges to the golden ratio

Call φ (phi) the golden ratio, roughly 1.6180339887. If the ratio F(n)/F(n−1) settles on some value x as n grows, then F(n+1)/F(n) also tends to that same x. But:

F(n+1)/F(n) = (F(n) + F(n−1))/F(n) = 1 + F(n−1)/F(n) = 1 + 1/x

So x must satisfy x = 1 + 1/x, which means:

x² − x − 1 = 0

Solving that quadratic gives exactly:

φ = (1 + √5) / 2

The golden ratio isn't an empirical observation about the sequence, then — it's a direct, inevitable consequence of the recurrence rule itself.

A formula that skips the steps

There's also a way to calculate the n-th Fibonacci number directly, without generating the whole sequence up to it: Binet's formula,

F(n) = (φⁿ − ψⁿ) / √5

where ψ = (1 − √5)/2 is the other solution of that same equation x² − x − 1 = 0. It seems surprising that a formula full of square roots and irrational powers always produces, exactly, a whole number — but it does: the irrational parts cancel each other out in the subtraction.
